## Overview

This module looked at how the world is trying to regulate AI. Major regional approaches in the US, EU, China, and Asia-Pacific. The relationship between regulation and innovation. Where every major jurisdiction stands on the global tracker.

---

## What I Learned

The biggest takeaway is that there is no global AI standard. Every major economy is moving at its own pace and with its own philosophy, which means a company building AI products today has to deal with a fragmented regulatory environment depending on where their users live.

The EU has taken the strictest and most comprehensive approach. The EU AI Act, adopted in 2024, is the world's first legally binding AI law. It uses a risk-based classification system. Some uses are flat-out banned, like social scoring by governments and cognitive behavioral manipulation of vulnerable groups, with the prohibition starting February 2, 2025. High-risk systems used in critical infrastructure, employment, education, law enforcement, and medical devices have to meet strict requirements around risk management, data governance, transparency, human oversight, and cybersecurity, with rules kicking in August 2, 2026. Limited-risk systems like chatbots only need to disclose they are AI. Minimal-risk applications like spam filters stay largely unregulated. Generative AI gets its own transparency layer, including labeling AI-generated content and publishing summaries of copyrighted training data. Penalties go up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of global revenue.

The US is the opposite story. There is no comprehensive federal AI law. Regulation is piecemeal, built from executive orders, agency guidance, and sector-specific rules. Biden's 2023 executive order on safe and trustworthy AI set a regulation-heavy tone but was not binding law. The new Trump administration is expected to roll most of that back, push agencies like the FTC and Commerce toward a hands-off posture, ramp up AI export controls aimed at China, and reduce antitrust enforcement on AI mergers and acquisitions. A January 2025 executive order titled "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence" already revoked prior directives the administration saw as restrictive. There is growing talk of federal preemption of state laws like the ones passed in Colorado, Utah, and California, on the argument that a patchwork of state rules raises compliance costs.

Texas sits in the middle of the federal vacuum. The Texas Responsible AI Governance Act, TRAIGA, is heavily inspired by the European framework. It classifies AI uses by risk, requires impact assessments for high-risk systems, and bans certain uses like social scoring and manipulative AI. If it passes, it will put Texas at the front of state-level AI policy in the US.

China takes yet another path. Instead of a single comprehensive law, China issues binding, targeted regulations focused on specific AI applications and on social stability. The 2022 rules on recommendation algorithms require transparency and user controls. The 2023 deep synthesis rules require watermarking and labeling of AI-generated content, especially deepfakes. The 2023 generative AI interim measures regulate public-facing services. New labeling rules effective September 1, 2025 require both visible and metadata-level labels on AI-generated content. The whole regime is wrapped around content governance, national security, and protecting what the regulations call "mainstream value orientations."

The rest of Asia-Pacific runs the full spectrum. Australia and Singapore have leaned on voluntary guidelines and ethics principles, with Australia now moving toward a risk-based approach inspired by the EU. Japan, South Korea, and Singapore emphasize innovation-friendly policies. Brazil has a risk-classification bill under review. South Korea has a Basic Act focused on risk assessments and human oversight for high-impact AI. India is developing AI policies focused on ethical growth and data protection. Russia is using Experimental Legal Regimes to test AI solutions like autonomous vehicles.

One of the most useful pieces in the materials was a European Parliamentary Research Service paper that pushed back on the assumption that regulation hinders innovation. The argument was that regulation and innovation are not zero-sum. The paper laid out four broad approaches policymakers can take: directly regulating AI development with carrots like moonshot funding and sticks like moratoria on biometric identification or autonomous weapons, shaping the broader context through capital, skills, data access, and competition policy, responding indirectly to harms as they emerge through worker protections and rights enforcement, and innovating in regulation itself through regulatory sandboxes, anticipatory governance, and regulatory markets where firms compete to meet regulator-defined demands. The point is that well-crafted regulation can actually be a precondition for trustworthy AI innovation rather than a barrier to it.

The global tracker surfaced five trends. AI is defined differently in every jurisdiction, which forces international businesses into a "highest common denominator" compliance strategy. AI regulations come in different legal forms, from EU regulations that apply directly across member states, to UK guidance interpreted by existing sector regulators, to a mix of US executive orders and state laws. The conceptual approaches vary too, with some jurisdictions making rules legally binding and others leaving them voluntary. Flexibility is a double-edged sword: high-level wording makes regulations adaptable to future technology but creates uncertainty for businesses planning long-term investments. AI regulation overlaps heavily with intellectual property, antitrust, data protection, financial regulation, and global trade, so even where there is no AI-specific law, AI deployments still have to clear a stack of adjacent legal hurdles.

International cooperation is happening through the G7, OECD, UN, the US-EU Trade and Technology Council, and the Global Partnership on AI. Progress is real but slow because national priorities, geopolitics, and the pace of the technology itself keep moving the target.

---

## Insights

There is no single global AI standard, and there probably will not be one for years. The EU is leading with a comprehensive risk-based framework that the rest of the world is partly copying and partly resisting. The US is taking the opposite approach, prioritizing innovation, national security, and competition with China over comprehensive rulemaking. China runs a third model focused on content control and social stability through targeted, binding rules.

Texas is becoming an interesting state-level case in the US because TRAIGA borrows directly from the EU playbook. If it passes, it positions Texas at the front of state-level AI policy.

The framing of regulation as the enemy of innovation is being challenged by serious policy research, which argues that the right kind of regulation is what makes trustworthy innovation possible in the first place.
